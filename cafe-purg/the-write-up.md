# Café Purg — The Write-Up

*A café and late-night non-alcoholic lounge in the Owl's Roost loft, Bentonville square.*
*Prepared by Evan Soos · June 2026 · Every number below is sourced in the full business case (`business-case.md`) and research files.*

---

## The idea in three sentences

There is no place in Northwest Arkansas for people between 18 and 21 — too old for curfew, too young for every bar on the square — and no late-night venue of any kind that isn't built on alcohol. Café Purg fills that gap with serious coffee, craft zero-proof drinks, and a membership model, in a space that already exists and stays open after everything else closes. No alcohol isn't a compromise; it's the moat — bars can't copy it without abandoning their license economics, and the NA beverage category just crossed **$1 billion** (+27%/yr).

Industry consensus for a seated café is **$80K–$300K** (median ~$150–200K) — we sit inside it. Two notes:
- **One menu decision saves ~$25–55K:** no fryer/grill/range. A ventless oven (~$12–18K) replaces a Type I hood system ($20–55K installed), keeps the health-inspection tier low, and likely shrinks the grease trap to an under-sink unit.
- **Permits are trivial:** all city + state fees for a $150K buildout total **~$1,300–$1,500** (city permit ~$730, health plan review capped at $500, food permit $35/yr, business registry free).

Membership pricing is proven, not invented: Panera charges $15/mo, independent drip clubs $29, espresso-inclusive programs $40–90, coworking desks $150. We price in the open middle ($25/$50 tiers).

## What the space is worth

Market rent for ~1,000 sq ft near the square is **$30–36/sq ft/yr = $30,000–$36,000 a year** (current downtown listings; UA Skyline Report; retail vacancy just 3.4%). A family lease below market is the difference between an industry-average café and a comfortably profitable one — **each $10K of rent relief is worth ~3 points of net margin**. And the shell work (HVAC, electrical, restroom, structure) is permanent property improvement that raises the building's value whether or not the café is forever.

## The regulatory path (verified against current code, June 2026)

- **Zoning: done before we start.** Bentonville's new code (effective May 14, 2026) makes "food and beverage" a **by-right use** on the square — no rezoning, no conditional use permit, **no parking minimums**.
- **The one real gate: the second floor.** The ADA's elevator exemption applies to buildings under 3 stories (the "shopping center" carve-out requires 5+ retail tenants; bookstore + café is two) — so an elevator is *plausibly not required*, but we get that **in writing** from the building official before spending anything. Fallback: a ground-floor pickup counter shared with the bookstore; worst realistic case, a platform lift at $10–25K.
- **The design trick:** keep seating under 50 occupants and the space is classed Group B, not Assembly — which **avoids the automatic sprinkler requirement** for second-floor assembly spaces. Under 30 occupants, the existing single stair may legally suffice.
- **Health department:** plan review before construction (≤$500), $35/yr permit, one ServSafe-certified manager (~$150). The current residential kitchen can't be used as-is — full conversion is required and is already in the budget above.
- **Timeline: 4–7 months** from hiring an architect to opening day.

## The sequence (designed so almost nothing is spent before the unknowns are answered)

1. **Pre-sell** founding memberships and run commissary-kitchen pop-ups (explicitly allowed by the health department) — target 75–100 committed members. *Cost: ~$0. This is the demand proof.*
2. **Free conceptual meeting** with city Planning and the Fire Marshal, together with Craig: parcel zoning, accessibility ruling in writing, stair/structural survey. *Cost: two hours.*
3. **Go / no-go.** If the answer is "elevator required" or "structural surprise," we stop — having spent essentially nothing.
4. Architect → health plan review → city permit → buildout → open.

## How it gets funded

A verified $250K capital stack: ~$60K founder/family equity (documented gifts count toward SBA requirements) + ~$100K SBA 7(a) through Arkansas Capital Corporation + ~$35K CDFI microloan (FORGE/Communities Unlimited) + **$15K from Kiva NWA at 0% interest, no credit score required, Walton-matched**. The biggest lever isn't a loan at all: Craig funding part of the fixed buildout as landlord TI, amortized into a market-rate rent (standard commercial structure, ~7–8%) — it shrinks the loan before it's sized, and SBA's related-party rules don't apply since no SBA money touches the building. Free help that lenders respect: ASBTDC loan packaging and Startup Junkie consulting.

## The alcohol question, answered

Researched against the ABC Rulebook and Arkansas Code directly. Three facts change the picture: **(1)** Arkansas has no rule excluding under-21 customers from a permitted *restaurant* — the legal wall we assumed between the 18–21 brand and alcohol doesn't exist; **(2)** the upgrade is cheap and unobstructed — Benton County is wet, the church-distance rule applies only to package stores, this block already holds five permits, and a Restaurant Beer & Wine permit is **$350/yr** with a 1–4 month process; **(3)** staying dry forgoes roughly **$25–45K/yr in gross profit** at base-case revenue.

The decision: **open zero-proof anyway** — an 18–21-branded venue pouring alcohol would be a permanent compliance target (sale-to-minor cases dominate ABC's enforcement docket), and the dry program is the one thing the five licensed neighbors can't copy. But for Craig the key point is the option value: **the tenant is never structurally capped — the $350 permit is exercisable in a single season** whenever the revenue data says so.

## Stated honestly

About half of independent cafés don't survive five years — and the comp set for "sober bars" specifically is worse: they fail or pivot routinely, and the survivors are *activity venues that happen to be alcohol-free*. That's why Café Purg is positioned as games + study + events + desserts with engineered per-head spend (evening target $15–22 with covers, vs. the $8–10 coffee ticket that sinks long-dwell venues), why membership matters (the slow-year stress test in `financials/` shows 100 members turning five losing winter months into zero), and why **nothing gets built until 75–100 founding members have actually paid**. The market data is favorable; the comp set is a warning; the pre-sale settles the argument either way — for a few thousand dollars instead of two hundred thousand.
